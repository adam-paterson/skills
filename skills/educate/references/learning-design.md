# Learning design patterns

Use these patterns selectively. The subject, learner model, desired performance, and delivery environment determine the method.

## Match the method to the learning

### Conceptual knowledge

Use contrasting examples, analogies with explicit limits, diagrams, self-explanation, classification tasks, and retrieval questions.

Evidence: learners explain the idea in their own words, distinguish examples from non-examples, predict consequences, or connect it to prior knowledge.

### Factual knowledge

Use concise explanations, organised reference material, retrieval practice, spacing, and application in meaningful contexts.

Evidence: learners recall the information when needed and use it correctly; recognition alone is weak evidence.

### Procedures and practical skills

Use demonstration, decomposition, worked examples, guided practice, immediate feedback, faded scaffolding, and repeated independent performance.

Evidence: learners perform the procedure under realistic conditions, diagnose errors, and recover without following a script.

### Judgment and decision-making

Use cases, comparisons, edge conditions, think-aloud modelling, trade-off discussions, prediction, and feedback from consequences or experts.

Evidence: learners choose an approach, explain their reasoning, notice relevant cues, and revise when evidence changes.

### Physical, interpersonal, or safety-critical performance

Use supervised practice, observation, rehearsal, simulation, and qualified human feedback. Slides and quizzes are insufficient evidence. Identify where professional instruction, safeguarding, certification, or controlled facilities are required.

## Build durable learning

- **Retrieval:** require reconstruction from memory instead of immediate rereading.
- **Spacing:** revisit important knowledge after delay rather than massing all practice together.
- **Interleaving:** mix related skills when learners must learn to select the right one; avoid it during initial explanation when it only adds confusion.
- **Variation:** change surface details while preserving the underlying principle.
- **Generation:** let learners attempt or predict before showing the solution when failure is safe and informative.
- **Feedback:** make it timely, specific, connected to the goal, and actionable.
- **Reflection:** ask what cue mattered, why an approach worked, and what would change in another context.

Do not confuse momentary fluency, confidence, participation, or enjoyment with durable learning.

## Manage difficulty and cognitive load

- Remove irrelevant complexity during initial explanation.
- Teach prerequisite elements before asking learners to coordinate them.
- Use worked examples for novices; fade support as competence grows.
- Break long explanations with decisions, predictions, retrieval, or practice.
- Keep reference information visible when memorisation is not the goal.
- Avoid decorative visuals that compete with the explanation.
- Use consistent terminology and record a glossary when the subject has specialised language.
- Provide an easier route and an extension route when the cohort is mixed.

Difficulty should come from the capability being learned, not confusing instructions or unnecessary memory demands.

## Sequence a programme

Build a prerequisite map rather than a topic list:

1. Identify the final authentic performance.
2. Decompose the performance into decisions, knowledge, and component skills.
3. Mark true prerequisites and common bottlenecks.
4. Find a small early performance that gives learners a tangible win.
5. Increase independence and realism over time.
6. Revisit earlier capabilities inside later tasks.
7. End with transfer to a less-scripted context.

Use one continuing problem, artifact, story, or scenario when it reduces setup cost and makes progress visible. Use varied cases when selection and transfer are the goals.

## Avoid common teaching failures

- **Topic dumping:** organising around everything the instructor knows rather than what learners need to do.
- **Slide-first design:** committing to a presentation before outcomes, evidence, and practice are clear.
- **Coverage as success:** reporting that content was mentioned without evidence that learners can use it.
- **Activity without purpose:** adding interaction that produces no relevant practice or feedback.
- **Copying as practice:** learners reproduce the instructor's exact example without choosing or adapting.
- **Assessment mismatch:** testing recall when the goal is performance or judgment.
- **False beginner assumptions:** using jargon or hidden prerequisites the learner model does not support.
- **Confidence theatre:** using self-rated confidence as the only measure of learning.
- **One-shot delivery:** failing to capture evidence and improve later sessions.
